## The five themes, in the students' own words

**1. Sustainability as a personal value.** Students arrived with their values. One described vegetarianism as "not just a lifestyle, but also a conscious choice". The cafeteria **reinforced** these values rather than creating them.

**2. Practical constraints decide.** Values lose to the wallet: "Sustainable food is more expensive… the money factor is important to me as a student." One student preferred the buffet for its quality but chose the set menu "because of the price difference".

**3. The cafeteria enables.** Students praised the modern, clean space, the vegetarian and vegan options, and the reusable takeaway boxes. Sustainable choices were easy to find, but **availability alone did not decide behaviour**.

**4. Communication blocks engagement.** "Everything is in German… I have to take a picture and translate." Another student had "no idea what their sustainable practices are". The practices existed; students simply could not see or read them.

**5. Social influence is situational.** Most students decided for themselves, but group lunches and seeing what others picked shaped choices on specific days.

## What a university could do with this

- **Show menus in more languages** on the existing screens, as one student suggested.
- **Introduce the cafeteria in the welcome programme** for international students, including its sustainability practices.
- **Make sustainable options the affordable default**, because values alone do not beat price and convenience.

## Limitations we named

Five interviews cannot represent all international students; answers were self-reported; and students were interviewed early in their move to Switzerland, so their habits may still change. A larger study could combine interviews with actual purchase data.

## What I learned

How to design an interview guide that gets real behaviour rather than polite opinions, how to code qualitative data step by step, and how to turn quotes into evidence a decision-maker can use.
