## Why this question matters

Food systems are responsible for about **a third of global greenhouse gas emissions** (Crippa et al., 2021), and what people eat is one of the few levers individuals control every day. Universities feed thousands of people daily, so their cafeterias shape habits at scale. Research also shows that the food environment itself changes choices: when Cambridge cafeterias offered more vegetarian options, the share of vegetarian meals sold rose (Garnett et al., 2019).

International students are a particularly interesting group. They arrive in a new food culture, with new prices, new products and often a new language, at exactly the moment their habits are open to change.

## How we did it

> We chose **problem-centred interviews** over focus groups. In a group, students tend to say what sounds socially acceptable about sustainability. One-to-one, they describe what they actually do.
{: .method}

Each of the five team members conducted one 45 to 60 minute interview with a newly arrived international student, recorded with consent and transcribed. We coded the transcripts with descriptive codes (what is being described) and in vivo codes (the student's own words), added codes that emerged from the data, and grouped them into five themes on a thematic map.

## The five themes, in the students' own words


**1. Sustainability as a personal value.** Students arrived with their values. One described vegetarianism as "not just a lifestyle, but also a conscious choice". The cafeteria **reinforced** these values rather than creating them.

**2. Practical constraints decide.** Values lose to the wallet: "Sustainable food is more expensive… the money factor is important to me as a student." One student preferred the buffet for its quality but chose the set menu "because of the price difference".

**3. The cafeteria enables.** Students praised the modern, clean space, the vegetarian and vegan options, and the reusable takeaway boxes. Sustainable choices were easy to find, but **availability alone did not decide behaviour**.

**4. Communication blocks engagement.** "Everything is in German… I have to take a picture and translate." Another student had "no idea what their sustainable practices are". The practices existed; students simply could not see or read them.

**5. Social influence is situational.** Most students decided for themselves, but group lunches and seeing what others picked shaped choices on specific days.


> Values were strong, but **perceived control** decided behaviour. This is the attitude-behaviour gap described by Vermeir and Verbeke (2006): people intend to buy sustainably, but price, convenience and information decide what they actually do.
{: .key}

## What a university could do with this

- **Show menus in more languages** on the existing screens, as one student suggested.
- **Introduce the cafeteria in the welcome programme** for international students, including its sustainability practices.
- **Make sustainable options the affordable default**, because values alone do not beat price and convenience.

## Limitations we named

Five interviews cannot represent all international students; answers were self-reported; and students were interviewed early after their move to Switzerland, so their habits may still change. A larger study could combine interviews with actual purchase data from the cafeteria.

## What I learned

How to design an interview guide that gets real behaviour rather than polite opinions, how to code qualitative data step by step, and how to turn quotes into evidence a decision-maker can use. The same skills now drive my healthcare and SME consulting projects.
