The weakest starting position is also by far the most common one. About **83% of new Swiss companies start with a single employee**, a share that has barely changed since 2013.

## Survival year by year

The chart shows how many companies of each size are still alive after one, two, three, four and five years. Notice where the lines separate:

<div class="chart-embed" data-chart="size" data-label="Survival curve by size at founding"></div>

The big jump is from **one person to a small team**: going from one employee to two to four adds about ten points of survival. Going from two to four to five to nine adds almost nothing. The message is not "the bigger the better". It is **"do not start alone"**.

## Capital at the start helps too

Legal form tells a similar story. A GmbH needs at least CHF 20,000 of share capital and an AG CHF 100,000, while a sole proprietorship needs nothing:

<div class="chart-embed" data-chart="legal" data-label="Survival by legal form"></div>

GmbHs (56%) and AGs (55%) survive better than sole proprietorships (48%). Foreign companies registered in Switzerland survive worst, at 35%, which connects back to [Zug in part 2](/blog/zug-founds-the-most-keeps-the-fewest/).

## Why small teams survive better

- **Capacity.** One person must sell, deliver, do the accounts and absorb every setback. One illness or one lost client can end the business.
- **Complementary skills.** A team can combine, for example, a product person and a sales person.
- **Commitment.** Hiring someone or putting in share capital signals a more serious, better-prepared project.

## The nuance: what the research says about solo founders

As a market researcher, I checked whether the wider evidence agrees, and it is more complicated than "teams win". A well-known study by Jason Greenberg (NYU) and Ethan Mollick (Wharton) of crowdfunded companies found that **companies started by solo founders survived longer** than those started by teams, and earned more than founder pairs.

These two results do not actually contradict each other, and the difference is worth understanding:

> The BFS data measures **employees at founding**, not the number of founders. A company with "one employee" is usually one person doing everything. A solo *founder* in the Greenberg and Mollick study could still build a team around them. The lesson is less "never found alone" and more **"do not try to run the whole company alone"**.
{: .note}

There is also a selection effect to keep in mind: founders who are better prepared may both hire earlier and survive longer. That is correlation, not proof that hiring alone causes survival.

## Why this matters now

AI tools make it easier than ever to start a company alone. In the US, about 36% of startups founded on Carta in 2025 had a solo founder, up from 31% a year earlier. Switzerland shows no shift yet, but if AI pushes more people to start completely alone, my data suggests survival would fall.

## What I would tell a founder

Find at least one other person early: a co-founder, a first employee, or a freelancer with complementary skills. Commit some capital, even if it is not legally required. And decide in advance which tasks you will **not** do yourself.
