When I researched AI for my study, I found headlines claiming that most companies succeed with AI, and others claiming that almost all fail. Both cite serious studies. As a market researcher, that is the moment to stop reading conclusions and start reading methods.

## Five studies, five answers

| Study | Who was asked | Success means | Window | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Google Cloud | Leaders at firms already using AI | Any ROI | First year | 74% achieve ROI |
| IBM | 2,000 CEOs in 33 countries | The ROI they expected | Recent years | 25% delivered |
| McKinsey | 1,993 participants | Any profit (EBIT) effect | Current | 39% see an effect |
| S&P Global | Companies in 2025 | Projects kept or dropped | One year | 42% dropped most |
| MIT NANDA | 300+ initiatives | Measurable P&L impact | Six months | 5% |

## Five reasons they disagree

1. **Who is in the sample.** A study that only asks companies already running AI never counts the projects that died before launch. That is survivorship bias.
2. **What counts as success.** "Any return" is far easier to reach than "the return we expected" or "a measurable effect on profit".
3. **The time window.** A project can look like a failure at six months and a success at eighteen.
4. **Who answers.** Executives rate projects they sponsored themselves. These are self-assessments, not measurements.
5. **Who publishes.** Some publishers sell AI products or AI transformation work. That does not make the data wrong, but a careful reader notes it.

## The hidden denominator

Even inside a single study, the headline depends on which line you pick:

{% include diagrams/funnel.html %}

"88% use AI" and "6% get significant value" are both true at the same time.

> If about half of AI projects die before production (S&P Global: 46% of proofs of concept scrapped), then a 74% success rate *among deployed projects* is closer to 40% of *all projects started*. This combines two samples, so read it as an illustration of the denominator problem, not a statistic.
{: .key}

## Why even a 25 to 30% failure rate matters

**Failures show where AI does not fit.** Klarna let an AI assistant handle around two-thirds of customer conversations, the work of about 700 agents. In May 2025 its CEO admitted the result was lower quality and began hiring humans again. The AI worked for routine questions and failed on complex ones.

**Portfolio versus single bet.** A large company can run forty pilots and accept that a third fail. A small Swiss firm usually gets one bet, and a failed rollout can cost staff, know-how and customers.

> A 70% success rate is fine for many small, reversible experiments, and dangerous for one large, irreversible bet. For small firms, the AI decision itself is a survival risk.
{: .remember}

## Four questions I now ask of any statistic

Who was counted? What counted as success? Over what time? Who published it? [Part 9](/blog/five-headlines-that-mislead/) applies the same questions to five Swiss headlines.
