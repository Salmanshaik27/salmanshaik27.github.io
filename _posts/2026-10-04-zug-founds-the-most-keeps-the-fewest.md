If you judged Swiss cantons by how many companies they create, Zug would win easily. It founds **13.7 new companies per 1,000 residents**, about 2.5 times Zurich's rate and more than three times Bern's. It also attracts more venture capital per resident than Zurich.

So I expected Zug's companies to do well. They do not.

## What the data shows

Zug had the lowest five-year survival of the five cantons I compared in **every founding year from 2013 to 2018**. That rules out bad luck or a single shock. Switch the chart below between cantons to see how consistent the pattern is:

<div class="chart-embed" data-chart="cantons" data-label="5-year survival by canton, founding years 2013 to 2018"></div>

Zug is also a revolving door. In 2021 it had both the highest rate of new companies (10.3 per 100 active companies) and the highest rate of closures (7.5 per 100). Companies arrive and leave faster than anywhere else in my sample.

## Testing the usual explanations

As a researcher, the temptation is to grab the first explanation that sounds right. Instead, I listed the common ones and checked each against the data.

**1. "It's all shell companies."** Rejected. The BFS survival statistics exclude holding companies and count only firms with real economic activity. Zug's low survival comes from operating companies.

**2. "Zug's founders start too small."** Rejected, and the data points the other way. Zug has the *fewest* one-person starts of the five cantons (77% against 83% nationally), and one-person starts are the weakest. On size alone, Zug should survive better.

**3. "Zug lacks money."** Rejected. Zug receives about CHF 2,026 of venture capital per resident, more than Zurich.

**4. "It's crypto."** Only partly. Zug is the heart of Crypto Valley, and the 2018 founding year lived through crypto crashes in 2018 and 2022. But Zug has about 715 blockchain companies in total, against roughly 1,800 new companies every year. Crypto cannot carry the whole effect.

> Before accepting an explanation, check whether it predicts what you actually see. "Shell companies" and "small starts" both sound plausible for Zug, but the data contradicts both.
{: .method}

## What probably explains it

Two explanations fit the evidence best, and I mark them clearly as hypotheses:

- **Foreign-owned companies.** Nationally, foreign companies registered in Switzerland survive only **35%** of the time, the lowest of any legal form. Zug's tax regime attracts many of them.
- **Mobile, tax-driven firms.** A company that came for the tax rate can leave for a better one. Low taxes attract companies that are, by design, easy to move.

Both point to the same idea: **location shapes survival mainly through the type of company a place attracts.**

## What this means

For a canton, the lesson is uncomfortable. If success is measured by registrations, Zug looks outstanding. If it is measured by companies that last and jobs that stay, it looks average at best. A better scorecard would track **survival rates and employment three and five years after founding**, not only new entries.

For a founder choosing where to register, low taxes are not a survival strategy. The question is what kind of customers, talent and network a location gives you.

> Zug maximises volume, not durability. A canton that measures success by registrations will celebrate exactly the wrong number.
{: .remember}

## What I would research next

The obvious next step is to split Zug's closures by ownership: how much of the churn comes from foreign-owned and mobile companies? BFS does not publish survival by canton and ownership together, so this would need a custom data request. It is on my list.
