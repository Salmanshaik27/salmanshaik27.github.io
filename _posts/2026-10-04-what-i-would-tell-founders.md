Twelve posts ago I asked why half of new Swiss companies close within five years. Here is the answer in one sentence, and what I would do with it.

> New Swiss companies mostly fail because of **how they start and which market they enter**, not because of where they are or how much money they raise.
{: .key}

{% include diagrams/driver-tree.html %}

## For founders

- **Do not run the company alone.** Find a partner, a first hire or a freelancer with complementary skills early. One-person starts survive 49%; small teams 59%. ([Part 5](/blog/why-solo-founders-close-more-often/))
- **Choose a market with a barrier.** A licence, a hard skill, a trusted relationship or a regulation protects you more than a fashionable sector. ([Part 4](/blog/health-vs-hospitality-sectors/))
- **Test demand before the irreversible bet.** No long lease or large contract until the concept is proven. ([Part 10](/blog/hospitality-retail-not-ai/))

## For SME owners

- **Put AI on the back office first**, with data rules in place before anyone pastes customer data into a tool. ([Part 7](/blog/ai-after-chatgpt-swiss-industries/))
- **Track cash runway and unit costs every month.** Most closures are slow, visible declines.
- **Share fixed costs with peers**: staff pools, joint purchasing, shared kitchens or workshops.

## For investors

- **Look past AI labels.** Ask what part of the value survives when AI tools get cheaper.
- **Non-AI startups now trade at lower valuations** because capital has moved to AI. For patient investors that can be an opportunity. ([Part 6](/blog/money-does-not-explain-survival/))

## For cantons

- **Measure survival and jobs, not registrations.** A record founding year can hide a record closure year. ([Part 2](/blog/zug-founds-the-most-keeps-the-fewest/))
- **Support years two and three**, not only the founding. That is when most companies fail.
- **Publish survival by canton and sector together**, so founders can see the real odds where they are.

## An agile playbook for small firms

My Scrum background shapes how I think about this. Agile methods were built for software, but their core idea fits any small business: **make change in small steps you can reverse, and inspect results often.**

1. **Pre-mortem.** Before a big commitment, imagine it failed a year from now and list why. Fix the top three reasons first.
2. **Smallest test first.** A pop-up instead of a lease, a pilot instead of a rollout, one client instead of a launch.
3. **Short cycles.** Review every two to four weeks and decide: continue, change or stop.
4. **Kill criteria in advance.** Decide before starting what result means stop.
5. **Readiness check.** Strategy, structure, systems, skills and people must all support the change, not just the technology (McKinsey's 7S).

> Agile turns one big, irreversible bet into many small, reversible experiments. That is exactly what a 70% success rate requires. ([Part 8](/blog/hidden-denominator-ai-success-rates/))
