In [part 1](/blog/why-sap-s4hana-projects-stall/) I showed that most S/4HANA projects miss their plan, and that five of the six biggest barriers are organisational. Two of them are about people directly: staff planned too late (75%) and organisational inertia (37%).[^horv][^asug]

The strongest lever in the evidence is change management. Prosci's research with more than 2,600 practitioners found that projects with excellent change management met or exceeded their objectives 88% of the time, against 13% with poor change management.[^prosci]

## Five steps to adoption

<figure><img loading="lazy" src="/assets/img/sap-adkar.png" alt="People adopt a new system in five steps: awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, reinforcement"/><figcaption><span>Prosci's ADKAR model applied to S/4HANA.</span><span class="credit">Diagram by Salman Shaik</span></figcaption></figure>

The steps build on each other. Training is wasted on people who do not understand why the change is happening. Practice fails when people are afraid to admit they are stuck. That is why training and a safe culture belong together.

## It is not a choice any more

For employees, S/4HANA is not an optional tool. Once the old system is switched off, the new one is the only way to book an order, close the month or ship a pallet. People who are not trained in time do not simply work more slowly. They block the next department.

A rhythm that works:

- **Everyone affected:** a short "why, and what changes for me" session at the start of each wave.
- **End users:** hands-on training in their own process, weekly from about eight weeks before go-live.
- **Key users, one per team:** deeper training, then they coach their colleagues.
- **After go-live:** floor support and a daily question slot for the first weeks.

## Leaders set the climate

Weekly training only works if people feel safe to say "I don't understand this yet". A manager who mocks a question teaches the whole team to stay quiet. Silent people build workarounds, workarounds turn into silos, and silos become the bottlenecks that stall the go-live. Harvard professor Amy Edmondson calls the opposite *psychological safety*: a team where asking for help carries no penalty.

> The fastest way to slow down a transformation is to make people ashamed of learning.
{: .key}

What leaders have to do:

1. **Decide the why** and say it in plain words: what gets better for customers, staff and the business.
2. **Explain the urgency honestly.** Support ends in 2027, and every year of delay costs extra fees and scarcer consultants.
3. **Free people up.** Key users cannot learn a new system and run the old business at full load.
4. **Support without judgement**, in every training session and every meeting.
5. **Protect wellbeing.** Go-live months mean double work. Plan realistic workloads, thank people visibly and set a clear end date for the extra load.
