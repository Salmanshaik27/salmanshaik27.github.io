Every year, Swiss newspapers report record numbers of new companies. It sounds like a healthy economy. But a founding boom only matters if the companies last, and when I followed one founding year all the way through, the picture changed: **for every two companies founded in Switzerland in 2018, one was gone by 2023.**

That raised the question I wanted to answer as a consultant would: not "how many companies are founded?", but **"which ones survive, and why?"**

## How I approached it

I treated this like a client question. Before touching any data, I broke the main question into five parts that do not overlap and together cover the whole problem, and I wrote down what I expected to find for each one.

{% include diagrams/issue-tree.html %}

Then I collected the official data: survival rates and company counts from the Federal Statistical Office (BFS) for every canton from 2013 to 2023, population figures to turn counts into fair comparisons, and the Swiss Venture Capital Report 2026 for funding. The tables came in German and Italian with merged headers, so I translated and reshaped them, and checked my totals against the published ones before trusting any result.

> I picked the 2018 founding year because it is the most recent one with a full five years of follow-up. BFS data runs about two years behind, so this is as fresh as a complete five-year view can be.
{: .method}

## Is 50.7% bad?

Not by European standards. Eurostat puts the EU five-year survival rate at about **45%** for companies founded in 2014. Switzerland does somewhat better. The point is not that Switzerland has a crisis, but that half of all founding effort still disappears, and that the losses are concentrated in predictable places.

## What I found

The five factors do not matter equally. Ranking them by the size of the survival gap they create gives a clear order:

{% include diagrams/driver-tree.html %}

1. **How a company starts matters most.** One-person starts survive 49%; companies starting with ten or more employees, 68%. Companies set up with share capital (GmbH, AG) beat sole proprietorships. [Read part 5](/blog/why-solo-founders-close-more-often/).
2. **Which market it enters comes next.** Health survives best at 63%, hospitality worst at 38%. The fashionable sectors, ICT and finance, are below average. [Read part 4](/blog/health-vs-hospitality-sectors/).
3. **Location matters less**, and mostly through the kind of company a place attracts. Zug founds 2.5 times more companies per resident than Zurich and keeps the fewest. [Read part 2](/blog/zug-founds-the-most-keeps-the-fewest/).
4. **Money does not explain survival.** This was the hypothesis I got wrong: Lucerne gets almost no venture capital and survives as well as Zurich. [Read parts 3](/blog/lucerne-survives-without-venture-capital/) and [6](/blog/money-does-not-explain-survival/).
5. **AI is not yet the main cause of failure**, but it is already changing which companies get founded and funded. [Read parts 7](/blog/ai-after-chatgpt-swiss-industries/) and [8](/blog/hidden-denominator-ai-success-rates/).

> New Swiss companies mostly fail because of **how they start and which market they enter**, not because of where they are or how much money they raise.
{: .key}

## Why this matters

For founders, it means the most important decisions happen before the first customer: whether to start alone, how much capital to commit, and whether the market has any barrier that protects you. For cantons, it means counting registrations celebrates the wrong number. For investors, it means venture capital touches a tiny share of the economy and cannot fix survival on its own.
